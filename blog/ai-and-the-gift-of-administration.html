AI and the Gift of Administration

A church planter's honest report on what a new tool can and cannot do

There is a verse most of us read past. In 1 Corinthians 12, after Paul lists apostles, prophets, and teachers, after miracles and healing, he includes two gifts that never make the highlight reel: helping and guidance, or as other translations put it, administrating (1 Corinthians 12:28). The Greek word behind it is kubernesis. It is the word for a ship's pilot, the person at the helm who takes the raw power of wind and current and turns it into a course.

That is a gift. Not a personality type, not a necessary evil that keeps the real ministry running. Paul puts it in the same list as prophecy and says God placed it in the church.

I want to talk about that gift, because I think it is the right place to start a conversation the church badly needs to have about artificial intelligence.

The gift God gave for growth

Administration is the gift that brings systems and order to teams, organizations, and endeavors so they can be healthy, sustainable, and able to grow. God has been giving it to people from the beginning. Joseph administrated a nation through famine (Genesis 41). Jethro looked at Moses trying to judge every dispute himself and told him to build a structure before he burned out (Exodus 18). The apostles, when the widows were being overlooked, did not preach harder. They organized, and the word of God spread (Acts 6:1-7).

God intends the church to use this gift in its mission of reaching the world with the gospel through church planting and disciple making. Order is not the opposite of the Spirit. Order is often how the Spirit's work gets to last past Sunday.

The church has always used tools

The church has never advanced the gospel without tools. Paul wrote letters and sent them down Roman roads, in the common Greek of the first century, so the gospel could reach the ends of the known earth. Gutenberg's press put Bibles in the hands of ordinary people. Today we use projectors, Bible apps, translation services, and quiet, creative gospel presentations that reach places a missionary cannot walk into.

Every one of those tools could be and has been used for evil. The same press that printed Bibles printed propaganda. The same roads carried soldiers. But we need to be careful about giving a tool moral status. Morality begins and ends in the hearts, hands, minds, and intentions of people made in the image of God. A tool in the hands of godly people is a good thing. It always has been.

The questions are fair

With the arrival of AI, a lot of ethical and practical questions have surfaced, especially in the church. What is okay? What is not? Where are the lines for using AI as a tool in ministry? Is this the beast system? Is this the antichrist?

These are valid questions and good things to wrestle through. I am not going to pretend to have settled all of them. What I can do is share my journey and where I have landed, in the specific context of planting a church.

My journey so far

Our team has used AI since we launched. In the early days it was ChatGPT, and honestly we used it like a glorified Google search and a thinking partner. It was helpful. It was not transformative.

Over the last three months, several of us, myself included, moved to a higher tier on Claude. If you lead a nonprofit and have not looked, most of these platforms offer 501(c)(3) discounts that are quite generous. I was able to move to a premium account for not much more than I had been paying for a standard ChatGPT subscription.

That change has genuinely revolutionized my ministry workflow, and the revolution has almost entirely been in the administrative work.

As a church planter I wear a lot of hats. I oversee production. I oversee finance. I oversee scheduling and team building. I sit in decision making and execution. I create content for preaching and for prayer, and for a season I built our virtual prayer meetings. I help with communication to our community and to our team.

In every one of those areas, AI has become an administrative juggernaut. It has massively increased my margin. Not only at work but at home. I have built dashboards for our family, grocery lists, and systems that help my wife and me track our nutrition and training. I am in the best shape of my life partly because of it. I revived old computers I thought were long dead, and my children now use them for school.

Here is the result that matters most to me. I show up on Sunday morning having accomplished more during the week, and I still have margin left in the tank to connect with people. The administrative load is lighter, so the relational part of the calling gets more of me, not less.

What it cannot replace

I want to be precise here, because this is where the fear lives.

AI cannot replace my communion with God. It cannot replace the secret place, my devotional time, worship with my family, or the friendships with the people I am running alongside. It cannot pray for me. It cannot fast for me. It cannot open the Word of God to my heart. Those things are mine to do, and they are the point of the whole thing.

But it can help me run payroll. It can keep the schedule. It can build out ProPresenter for Sunday. It can hand me dashboards more excellent than anything I could have made with my own administrative skill and time. Every one of those tasks serves, builds, and strengthens the mission of planting this church.

That is the gift of administration, showing up through a new tool.

The storm and the sovereignty

I am not naive about the risk. There is a real threat and a looming, unknown storm on the horizon from these tools falling into the wrong hands. I do not know what that will look like, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

But I do know this. Nothing happens outside God's sovereignty. Before the foundations of the earth He saw this moment, the way He saw the Roman roads and the printing press. He is not surprised by it, and He is not wringing His hands. Tools like these, in the hands of godly people committed to advancing the gospel, discipling the nations, and preparing the earth for the Lord's return, are something He saw coming and something He can use.

Where I have landed

So I come back to where I started. Tools created by people made in God's image, tools that line up with the giftings Scripture names, administration among them, are not things the church ought to hold at arm's length. I think we ought to go all in, and filter every bit of it through a heart of discernment, integrity, and godliness.

Not all in on the tool as savior. All in on stewarding the gift God already gave us, with every means He has put in front of us.

The pilot at the helm still needs the wind. But he would be a fool to refuse a better rudder.

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:28; Genesis 41; Exodus 18; Acts 6:1-7 (NIV).
