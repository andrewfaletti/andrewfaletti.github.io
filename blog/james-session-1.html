Trials Are Spiritual Resistance Training

The Perseverance of Wisdom and Love

Watch the message above · or read the reflection below

Nobody signs up for hardship. When trouble hits — a diagnosis, a layoff, a strained relationship, a season where nothing seems to work — joy is about the last thing on the list of what we're feeling. So it's a little jarring that the very first thing James says to a community of struggling believers is this:

"Consider it nothing but joy, my brothers and sisters, whenever you fall into all sorts of trials." — James 1:2

Notice James doesn't say if you fall into trials. He says whenever. For the people he was writing to, "all sorts of trials" weren't the exception — they were the normal texture of life. And that's still true. So the question isn't whether trials will come. It's what we'll do with them when they do.

Joy is something you reckon, not something you wait to feel

The word translated "consider" is hēgeomai — an accounting word, a reckoning word. It's the language of adding up a column and arriving at a total. That matters, because it means joy here is a deliberate act of the will, not a feeling you sit around waiting for.

James isn't asking us to fake happiness or pretend a trial doesn't hurt. He's asking us to reckon — to look past the immediate pain and count, by faith, the outcome waiting on the other side. Trials are genuinely unpleasant and genuinely profitable at the same time. Both are true. The trick is learning to see the second one while you're still standing in the first.

The Greek word for the trials themselves, peirasmois, means a proving — the testing of a person's fidelity, integrity, and constancy. A trial isn't random noise. It's a proving ground.

What the testing actually produces

"The testing of your faith produces endurance. Let endurance have its perfect effect, so that you will be complete, not lacking anything." — James 1:3–4

"Testing" here is dokimion — a word that describes demonstrating the true quality of something as it passes through fire. The true character of a Christian isn't revealed in comfort. It's demonstrated as they pass through the trial. And on the far side of that testing is maturity: complete, whole, deficient in nothing.

Here's the image I keep coming back to.

The resistance-training principle

Think about what actually happens when you lift something heavy.

Your muscles

Resistance creates micro-tears in the muscle fiber. Those tears aren't the enemy — they're the signal. Strength gets rebuilt in the recovery, in the very place the muscle broke down. No resistance, no tearing, no rebuilding, no strength.

Your mind

The research on this is remarkably consistent: hard physical effort lowers anxiety, improves sleep, and produces the earned confidence that comes from doing a difficult thing you genuinely doubted you could do.

Your life

Chosen difficulty tends toward a longer, fuller life — healthspan, not just lifespan. Nutritional discipline works the same way. The thing that feels like deprivation in the moment is building something you can't yet see.

In every one of these, the pattern is identical: comfort produces atrophy; resistance embraced with wisdom produces strength. A muscle that's never challenged wastes away. A life that's never stretched does too.

That's the picture James is painting. Trials are spiritual resistance training. The weight is real. The strain is real. But so is the strength being built in the place where you feel like you're breaking down.

Wisdom is what lets you see it

Of course, none of that is automatic. Resistance can build you up or it can break you — and the difference is often wisdom. Which is exactly where James goes next:

"If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to him." — James 1:5

Wisdom does two things here.

It reframes the trial. Wisdom teaches us to interpret suffering — to see past the immediate pain to what God is producing through it. This is the same heavenly perspective Jesus had on the cross: "for the joy set before Him He endured the cross." He saw the outcome, and it changed how He carried the weight.

It comes without shame. "Without finding fault" means God doesn't scold you for not having it figured out yet. You don't have to arrive with your act together. You come with empty hands and ask — and He gives generously.

This is the fork in the road. Two people can walk through the identical trial and come out completely differently, because they're seeing it through completely different lenses:

The earthly lensThe heavenly lens
Trials are interruptionsTrials are formation
Preserve comfortPursue maturity
Self-protectionLove and mercy
Double-mindednessWholehearted devotion

One lens asks, how do I make this stop? The other asks, what is this making me?

The reward, and what makes endurance possible

"Blessed is the one who perseveres under trial, because having stood the test, that person will receive the crown of life that the Lord has promised to those who love him." — James 1:12

The ultimate reward for faithful, persevering love toward God is the glory of eternal life — the crown of life. But notice who the crown is promised to: "those who love him." That's not incidental. Paul writes that "love… always perseveres" (1 Corinthians 13:7).

Trials expose what's really inside us — our desires, our fears, our idols, our false comforts. Pressure reveals allegiance. And the thing that actually enables a person to endure isn't gritted teeth or willpower. It's a relationship of love with God. You persevere through the storm for the One you love, and the loving is what makes the enduring possible.

A life under trial: Helen Roseveare

If you want to see this lived out, look at Helen Roseveare.

She was a missionary doctor in the Congo — the founder of hospitals and rural clinics serving mothers, children, and people affected by leprosy. (If you want to know what "pure and undefiled religion" looks like, James 1:27 has an answer, and Helen is a photograph of it.) During the 1964 Simba Rebellion she was imprisoned and brutalized. And yet she would later describe that suffering as a privilege — a share in the sufferings of Christ. Then, in 1966, she went back. She returned to the very place of her trauma to rebuild the medical work, and she finished her life full of joy.

Here's what strikes me most, though: you don't have to go to the Congo to live like Helen. Through a faithful, loving relationship with God, she received the wisdom to face her trials — and she let those trials make her more faithful instead of more bitter. That same relationship, and the same wisdom it produces, is available to every single one of us, right where we are.

This week

Two invitations to carry out of this.

Pick one small storm. One goal, one hard thing you've been avoiding, and run at it this week. Let the win — even a small one — remind you that resistance is precisely how God builds strength.

And the bigger question: Where do you need wisdom and love to reframe the trials of your life — past, present, or future?

"But if anyone is deficient in wisdom, he should ask God… and it will be given to him." — James 1:5
