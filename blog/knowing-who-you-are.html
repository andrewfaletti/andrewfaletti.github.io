Knowing Who You Are

A devotional on the stewarded self

I want to talk about self. Specifically, about the difference between stewarding a self well and being run by one poorly.

We live in a moment that pushes us toward two ditches. One ditch is narcissism, making self the center, the loudest voice, the thing everyone else must orbit. The other ditch is being so afraid of that first ditch that we erase ourselves entirely. We dissolve into whatever the group wants, whatever keeps the peace, whatever stops people from being upset with us. Both are failures of self. One inflates it. The other abandons it.

Edwin Friedman, in A Failure of Nerve, offers a third way. He calls it self-differentiation. A differentiated leader is someone who knows who they are, is clear about their own values and direction, can stay calmly connected to people they disagree with, and can hold steady when the people around them get anxious and try to pull them off course. Friedman's word for it is a non-anxious presence. It is not detachment, and it is not selfishness. It is the strength to be a self while staying in relationship.

The model: Jesus

The clearest picture of this is Jesus.

Think about how often Jesus simply withdrew. Mark tells us, "Very early in the morning, while it was still dark, Jesus got up, left the house and went off to a solitary place, where he prayed" (Mark 1:35). And when the disciples finally track him down, notice their tone: "Everyone is looking for you!" (Mark 1:37). There is pressure in that sentence. The crowd wants him. The demand is real. And Jesus does not take the bait. He says, in effect, "Let's go somewhere else." Luke tells us it was his pattern: "Jesus often withdrew to lonely places and prayed" (Luke 5:16).

That frustrated people. It looked, at times, like he was moving too slow, going the wrong direction, not meeting the obvious need. But Jesus was not defined by the crowd's anxiety or the crowd's agenda. He knew who he was and whose he was. Because he had stewarded himself, his time with the Father, his sense of mission, he could stay present to enormous need without being consumed by it. He healed, and he also walked away to pray. He served everyone, and he belonged to no one's panic. That is a differentiated self.

Another witness: David

Before he faces Goliath, Saul tries to dress David in Saul's own armor. David tries it on, and then takes it off: "I cannot go in these, because I am not used to them" (1 Samuel 17:39). That is a small verse with a big backbone. David would not fight as someone he wasn't. He knew his own shape. He went as himself, with his sling, and that self, offered to God, was enough. He did not let someone else's expectation, or someone else's armor, redefine who he was.

The point

Stewarding self is not the same as serving self. Both Jesus and David show us that the healthiest leaders are the ones who are most rooted, not most reactive. Narcissism makes self the center. Dissolving makes self disappear. Differentiation does something harder and holier: it keeps a clear, God-anchored self in the room, calm and connected, even when everyone else is anxious.

Questions to sit with

Here are three questions I've been sitting with myself. I'd invite you to as well.

Where am I letting other people's anxiety set my direction? Is there a place right now where "everyone is looking for you" is driving me, more than a clear sense of who God made me to be?
What is my equivalent of "withdrawing to a solitary place"? How am I actually stewarding my self, my soul, my rest, my time with God, so that I have a self to bring to my team? Or am I running on empty and calling it faithfulness?
Where do I let the demands of others crowd out what matters most? Am I so quick to help everyone else, to answer every "everyone is looking for you," that I never tend to the things God has actually made most important for me to steward? Where am I helping others before I've stewarded myself?

Take a moment of quiet with these. That is usually where the real answer shows up, not in the reading, but in the silence right after.

Sources and anchors: Edwin H. Friedman, A Failure of Nerve: Leadership in the Age of the Quick Fix (self-differentiation, non-anxious presence, sabotage, overfunctioning). Scripture: Mark 1:35-38; Luke 5:16; 1 Samuel 17:38-40.
